The Third Story

— ✦ —

There are three stories about what happened on Lifeboat 10 that night: the one the newspapers printed, the one Percival H. Lytton told me in an isolated cottage seven years later, and the one I should have written but didn't.

I went to Ireland in December 1919 to get the first of those stories.

I'd come off the Fishguard ferry the day before, and the police were watching every passenger. One constable in the RIC took rather too long with my papers—civil enough, but his eyes were cold as stone. I spent the night in a wretched inn near the harbour and caught the morning train south at half-past seven. An Englishman travelling alone had reason to be careful. The locals had long memories and short tempers. I couldn't say I blamed them.

At Enniscorthy station yard, a jarvey offered to take me for five shillings. We rattled out of town through a fine, steady rain, the motor labouring up the hills, and not a word from the driver. Cattle stood in the fields, boxed in by low stone walls, as they always are in that country. The road narrowed, and after a couple of bends a cottage came into view: whitewashed, low-roofed, with ragged thatch, not a neighbour within shouting distance. No wreath at the door, no light in the window—and this three days before Christmas.

— 1 —

"English, is he?" the driver asked.

"So I'm told."

He took my five shillings and counted them carefully. "I'll be at the crossroads in an hour. You can walk the quarter-mile back." And with that he turned the motor round and left me standing in the lane.

I lit a cigarette and walked up to the cottage. The sort of place a man goes to ground. I knocked.

The door opened a crack. A thin pale face peered out.

"Yes?"

"Mr Ashton? Maitland, Morning Post. Doing a piece about Englishmen who stayed on after the Rising. Your name came up."

"I'm afraid I've nothing to say."

"Five minutes of your time."

He opened the door just wide enough and I stepped inside. The room was bare as a cell: table, two chairs, turf smouldering in the hearth, shotgun in the corner, heavy bolt on the door. No decorations, no sign of the season. A man expecting trouble.

"How long have you been here, Mr Ashton?"

"Seven years." He was still standing by the door. "What paper did you say?"

"The Morning Post."

I took the chair by the fire without waiting to be asked and opened my notebook. He watched me, then took the other chair. I led him through the usual patter—the rain, the state of the roads, the price of turf. Short answers, eyes never leaving my face. You know the breed—the sort of chap whose guilt you can smell across a room.

— 2 —

I let the small talk die and put my notebook aside.

"Right then," I said. "Let's stop wasting time. Your real name is Percival H. Lytton."

His eyes flicked to the shotgun in the corner, then back to me. Whatever calculation he made took less than a second. Then he rose and poured two measures of whisky. Handed one to me, then sat down with the other, his hand steady enough.

He took a sip. "How did you find me?"

"Chap from the Carpathia spotted you in Wexford two years back. Word gets around."

"I suppose it does." He took another sip. "What is it you want?"

"The truth. I know what Mrs Sterling said."

He looked at me for a long moment, then stared at the grate in the hearth.

"She sat beside me in the boat. Even in the dark, she knew."

"Tell me what happened."

"I was in First Class. Cabin B-58. I felt the collision around half-past eleven. I didn't realise at the time it was an iceberg."

"You went on deck?"

"Eventually. I saw them loading the boats. Women and children only, they said. Very firm about it."

"So you went back below."

"To C deck. A woman travelling alone—I'd seen her at dinner. Waited until she went on deck, then took what I needed from her cabin. A dress. Her fur coat. A wide-brimmed hat."

— 3 —

"Where did you change?"

"The gentlemen's lavatory." He said it without hesitation. "Then I went up and got into Lifeboat 10."

"And they discovered you in the boat?"

"When sailors were being transferred to another boat. My hat slipped. They asked who I was. I didn't answer."

"The newspapers found you in New York?"

"They chased me for a week. I told them my name was Ashton. They couldn't prove otherwise—I'd left all my papers in my cabin."

"So you ran."

"I took a freighter to Montreal. Made my way here eventually. The Irish don't ask questions."

I watched him across the table. His thin, pale face showed something I hadn't expected—not shame, but a kind of worn patience.

"You're a coward," I said.

"Yes."

The turf hissed in the grate. I had expected him to argue the point.

"You should have stayed. Helped with the boats. Gone down like a gentleman."

"I chose not to." He leaned back in his chair. "Tell me, did you see service in the war, Mr Maitland?"

"I did."

"France?"

"Ypres. The Somme."

— 4 —

"Ever choose to run? When the shells came down, when they told you to go over—ever think about turning back?"

I didn't care for the question. Any man who'd been through it knows the answer.

"That was different."

"Was it? Men dying around you. Death coming whether you stayed or ran. Only difference was they'd shoot you for it in France. On the Titanic, I had a choice."

I reached for my cigarettes and offered him one. He took it. We sat there smoking, two Englishmen in an Irish cottage, discussing cowardice.

"The boat was half-empty," he said. "Would it have been nobler to drown? Who would that have saved?"

"It's the rule," I said. "What decent men do."

"First-class men survived at higher rates than third-class women. Officers saved themselves. Men bribed the crew for places. And you want me to be the villain?"

"You could have helped. Done something useful."

I finished my cigarette and stubbed it out, the whisky gone, then closed my notebook and stood.

"Well," I said. "I appreciate your time, sir." I left it at that and moved toward the door.

"Mr Maitland," he said, rising. "Before you go. One thing you should know."

I turned, hand on the latch.

— 5 —

"I had every right to wear women's clothing that night."

"So you've said."

"No." His voice was quiet. "You misunderstand. I was born a woman, Mr Maitland. Still am, according to the doctors and lawyers. I'd lived as a man long before that ship went down."

I don't mind telling you, that stopped me cold. I let go of the latch and looked at him properly for the first time—the thin face, the narrow hands, the voice I'd thought too refined. I'd come expecting a coward and found something I had no name for. He must have seen it in my face.

"I was simply taking what men like you insist I still am. Women and children first—wasn't that the rule?"

"But you've lived as—" I couldn't quite finish it. "All these years. Why?"

"I made a choice, Mr Maitland. As you did when you went over the top. We all choose how we live with what we are."

"That's hardly the same thing."

"Isn't it?" he said. "You followed orders, knowing they might kill you. I broke the rules I was born to. They would have destroyed me. We both did what we had to."

— 6 —

I decided to let him have the last word—after all, some men need it more than others. I opened the door and stepped into the yard and lit another cigarette. The rain had let up, but the sky was still grey and low.

"Mr Maitland."

I stopped.

"I chose to live as a man. That night I took the one advantage I was born with. A seat in a boat. Write that."

I looked back at him standing in the doorway. For a second or two the right name escaped me.

"Goodbye, Mr Lytton."

I walked back down the lane where the jarvey was waiting at the crossroads, motor idling, the same surly fellow who'd brought me out—these Irish know how to nurse a silence. We rattled back to Enniscorthy through the dusk and I caught the evening train north.

By the time we reached Rosslare it was dark, and I was glad of it, having a story in the bag, right enough—though which story that was I couldn't say. A coward who saved himself, or a woman who chose to live as a man, or something that made nonsense of both. It was the sort of question an Englishman needs a large brandy to answer. You'll have worked out by now which one I told.

Happy Christmas.
